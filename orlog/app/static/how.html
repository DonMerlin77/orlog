What it actually looks like

Condensed from real runs. Questions were messy. Answers weren't.

You
I feel like I'm constantly behind. I'm working all the time but nothing's actually moving forward.
Loki
When you say nothing's moving forward — is that about a specific project, or more of a general feeling that the work isn't compounding into anything?
You
General I think. I have a business I'm building but I keep getting pulled into tasks that feel urgent but probably aren't.
Loki
What does a day look like when you feel like it actually moved something? Not a perfect day — just one where at the end you thought "okay, that mattered."
You
Honestly? When I shipped something, even small. Or had a real conversation with a potential customer. The rest of it kind of blurs.
Loki
So the question isn't really "why am I behind." The question is: what's pulling you away from the two things that actually feel like progress — shipping and talking to customers — and why do you keep saying yes to it? Ready to take that to Orlog?
Orlog — 9 perspectives argued this
The surface question ("why am I behind?") and the real question ("why do I keep choosing reactive work over the two things that move my business?") need different answers — and you've been solving the wrong one.
Verdict
You're not behind on work. You're behind on decisions about what work counts. Every day you do reactive tasks without a rule for why they come before shipping or customers, you're making a choice that feels like no choice. The work isn't the problem. The missing rule is.
ᚾ What to do next
  1. Write down the two things Loki named — shipping and customer conversations. These are your non-negotiable dailies.
  2. Before any reactive task, ask one question: does this have to happen before I do those two things today? If no, it moves to tomorrow.
  3. Run this rule for 5 days and notice what actually falls off the list on its own.
Condensed — real sessions run 5–8 exchanges
You
I keep starting things and never finishing them. Is that a me problem or a systems problem?
Orlog — 9 perspectives argued this
Contrarian: You don't have a finishing problem. You keep picking projects that aren't worth finishing. Fix your selection criteria, not your willpower.

Advocate: You started these with explorer energy, then held yourself to finisher standards. You've been blaming yourself for not completing things you never actually committed to.

Clinician: "Why don't I finish?" is the surface question. "What would make something worth finishing?" is the real one. Those need different answers.
Verdict
Pick a rule before you start: Explorer mode (begin fast, quit freely, no guilt) or Finisher mode (commit to it, ship it, quitting counts as failure). You've been playing Explorer while grading yourself as a Finisher. That's the whole problem.
ᚾ What to do next
  1. Look at your last 3 unfinished projects and label each one: was it Explorer or Finisher when you started it?
  2. For your current active project, write down which mode it is and what "done" actually means before you work on it today.
  3. If it's Explorer mode, set a kill date now — the day you either ship a minimum version or officially abandon it with no guilt.
Direct Orlog — no Loki needed when the question is already clear
You
We have $500 left in budget. Do we spend it on paid ads or on better product photos?
Orlog — 9 perspectives argued this
Paranoid: You don't actually know why visitors aren't converting. It could be photos. It could also be trust signals, price, or checkout friction. Spending $500 without checking is just a more hopeful form of blind spending.

Contrarian: Paid ads amplify whatever conversion rate you have. If your rate is near zero, you're just paying to send more people to a broken funnel.

Engineer: You have proof of concept — visitors exist. Your problem is conversion, not discovery. That changes the math entirely.
Verdict
Spend it on product photos — but only after a 3-day check to confirm photos are actually your bottleneck. Look at your heatmaps, check where visitors drop off, read your reviews. If the evidence points to photos, spend it. If it points somewhere else, you just saved $500 from solving the wrong problem.
ᚾ What to do next
  1. This week: pull your store's drop-off data — where exactly are visitors leaving? Product page, cart, or checkout?
  2. Read your last 10 customer reviews or support messages. Is photo quality mentioned, or is something else coming up?
  3. If both point to photos, book the photographer. If they point elsewhere, bring that finding back to Orlog.
